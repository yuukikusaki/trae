## Article 100: The Art of Sculpture

Sculpture, three-dimensional art, engages space, light, and material in ways two-dimensional media cannot. Sculptors carve, model, cast, or construct, transforming raw materials into objects of aesthetic, religious, or commemorative significance. Works range from intimate cameos to monumental public installations.

Carving, the subtractive method, removes material to reveal form within. Stone carving employs hammers, chisels, and rasps. Marble, prized for its translucency and workability, has adorned temples and palaces since antiquity. Wood carving uses softer tools and accepts different aesthetic qualities than stone.

Modeling builds form additively, pressing clay or wax into shapes. Terracotta, fired clay, produces durable sculptures from ancient China to contemporary studios. Bronze casting replicates models through lost-wax processes. Michelangelo preferred marble, claiming his figures already existed within the stone block.

Modern sculpture expanded materials and methods. Assemblage combines found objects into compositions. Installation art transforms entire spaces. Kinetic sculptures incorporate movement. Digital fabrication enables forms impossible through traditional means. Sculpture continues evolving as artists explore new materials, technologies, and conceptual approaches.

## Article 101: The Biology of the Immune System

The immune system defends against pathogens through layered defenses. Physical barriers including skin and mucous membranes prevent initial invasion. Coughing, sneezing, and vomiting expel intruders. Antimicrobial secretions, such as lysozyme in tears, destroy bacteria directly.

Innate immunity provides immediate, non-specific responses. Phagocytic cells engulf and digest pathogens. Natural killer cells destroy infected or cancerous cells. Complement proteins puncture pathogen membranes. Inflammatory responses recruit additional defenses to infection sites.

Adaptive immunity develops over days but generates specific, lasting protection. B cells produce antibodies matching antigens, pathogen-specific molecular signatures. Antibody functions include neutralization, opsonization, and complement activation. T cells either help B cells (helper T) or kill infected cells directly (cytotoxic T).

Memory enables rapid secondary responses. Vaccinations generate memory without disease. Immunodeficiency, whether inherited or acquired through HIV infection, leaves patients vulnerable to opportunistic infections. Autoimmune diseases result from immune attacks on self-tissues. Immunotherapy harnesses immune mechanisms to treat cancer and other conditions.

## Article 102: The Geography of Forests

Forests cover approximately thirty percent of Earth's land area, hosting the majority of terrestrial biodiversity. Tropical rainforests near the equator feature the highest species richness, with millions of species per hectare. Canopy layers create distinct habitats, from ground-dwelling mammals to canopy specialists.

Temperate forests experience seasonal temperature variation. Deciduous trees shed leaves in autumn, conserving water during cold winters. Coniferous taiga stretches across northern latitudes, adapted to extreme cold. Mediterranean forests feature drought-tolerant evergreen oaks and pines.

Forest ecology involves complex interactions. Mycorrhizal fungi extend root systems, exchanging nutrients for plant sugars. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria enrich soils. Predator-prey dynamics regulate herbivore populations. Canopy gaps trigger successional dynamics as light reaches the forest floor.

Deforestation releases stored carbon and destroys habitat. Agricultural expansion, logging, and mining drive forest loss. Tropical deforestation particularly threatens species extinction and climate change. Conservation strategies include protected areas, sustainable forestry, and agroforestry systems that integrate trees with agriculture.
