## Article 73: The Technology of Robotics

Robotics, the interdisciplinary field combining engineering and computer science, designs machines that perform tasks autonomously or with human guidance. Industrial robots automate manufacturing processes, performing welding, painting, assembly, and inspection with precision and speed exceeding human capabilities. Articulated robotic arms with multiple joints manipulate objects in three-dimensional space.

Autonomous mobile robots navigate environments using sensors including cameras, lidar, and ultrasonic rangefinders. Simultaneous localization and mapping algorithms construct environment representations while tracking robot position. Self-driving vehicles apply these techniques to urban streets, integrating perception, prediction, and planning systems.

Service robots assist humans in homes, hospitals, and public spaces. Vacuum-cleaning robots autonomously map and clean floors. Surgical robots enable minimally invasive procedures with enhanced dexterity. Social robots provide companionship and cognitive assistance to elderly individuals.

Artificial intelligence drives advances in robot perception and decision-making. Machine learning enables robots to generalize from training data, adapting to novel situations. Collaborative robots, designed to work safely alongside humans, share workspaces without barriers. Ethical considerations arise regarding automation's impact on employment and the safety of autonomous systems operating in human environments.

## Article 74: The Science of Nutrition Science

Nutritional science investigates the roles of food components in supporting health and preventing disease. Macronutrients, including carbohydrates, proteins, and fats, provide energy and building materials. Carbohydrates range from simple sugars rapidly absorbed in the bloodstream to complex starches and fibers that nourish beneficial gut bacteria.

Proteins supply amino acids, nine of which are essential and must be obtained from diet. Complete proteins from animal sources contain all essential amino acids, while plant proteins may require complementary combinations. Protein quality is assessed through digestibility and amino acid composition. Athletes and elderly individuals have elevated protein requirements for muscle synthesis and preservation.

Micronutrients, vitamins and minerals, enable metabolic reactions despite being required in tiny quantities. Deficiency diseases such as scurvy, rickets, and iron-deficiency anemia result from inadequate intake. Excess intake of certain vitamins, particularly fat-soluble varieties, can cause toxicity. Phytochemicals, non-nutrient plant compounds, provide antioxidant and anti-inflammatory benefits.

Dietary guidelines recommend balanced consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats while limiting added sugars, sodium, and saturated fats. Personalized nutrition considers genetic variation, gut microbiome composition, and metabolic individuality. Understanding nutrition empowers individuals to make informed dietary choices that support long-term health.

## Article 75: The History of Photography

Photography, invented in the nineteenth century, transformed how humans perceive and document reality. The camera obscura, a dark chamber with a small aperture projecting an inverted image, predates photography by centuries. Nicéphore Niépce captured the first permanent photograph in 1826, requiring eight hours of exposure. Louis Daguerre refined this process into the daguerreotype, enabling shorter exposures and clearer images.

George Eastman introduced the Kodak camera in 1888, democratizing photography with the slogan "you press the button, we do the rest." Flexible film rolls replaced glass plates, making cameras portable and accessible. Color photography emerged in the early twentieth century through Autochrome and later Kodachrome processes.

Digital photography, developing since the 1970s, captures images using electronic sensors rather than chemical reactions. Pixels, discrete light-sensitive elements, record scene intensity and color. Image processing software enables adjustments impossible in traditional darkrooms. Smartphone cameras have placed sophisticated photography capabilities in billions of pockets.

Photography serves journalism, science, art, and personal memory. Photojournalists document history, from wars to sporting triumphs. Scientific imaging ranges from electron microscopy to satellite reconnaissance. Fine art photographers create expressive works exhibited in galleries and museums worldwide. Photography continues evolving as computational techniques generate images beyond traditional optical limits.
