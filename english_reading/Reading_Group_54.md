## Article 160: The Science of Radiology

Radiology employs imaging to visualize internal structures without invasive procedures. X-rays pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by bone, revealing fractures and densities. Discovered in 1895, X-rays revolutionized medicine within months of Roentgen's announcement.

Computed tomography creates cross-sectional images from multiple X-ray measurements. CT excels at detecting tumors, hemorrhages, and trauma. Contrast agents enhance vascular visualization. Radiation dose concerns limit appropriate use.

Magnetic resonance imaging uses strong magnetic fields and radio waves to generate detailed soft tissue images. MRI causes no ionizing radiation and provides excellent contrast between tissues. Claustrophobia and contraindications with metallic implants limit some applications.

Ultrasound uses sound waves reflected at tissue interfaces. Obstetric ultrasound safely monitors fetal development. Echocardiography evaluates heart structure and function. Interventional radiology performs procedures using imaging guidance, including angioplasty and biopsies.

## Article 161: The Geography of Wetlands

Wetlands, transitional between aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems, include marshes, swamps, bogs, and fens. They occur where water saturates soils, creating anaerobic conditions favoring specialized plants. Wetlands occur along coastlines, river floodplains, and interior depressions.

Hydrological functions make wetlands invaluable. They store floodwaters, reducing downstream flooding. Soils and vegetation filter pollutants, improving water quality. Carbon sequestration in wetland soils stores atmospheric carbon.

Biological productivity rivals rainforests. Dense vegetation supports food webs from algae to mammals. Migratory birds depend on wetlands for nesting, feeding, and resting. Fish use wetlands as spawning habitat.

Draining wetlands for agriculture has destroyed approximately seventy percent of historical wetlands globally. Conservation protects remaining areas through protected designations and restoration projects. Wetland mitigation banking allows development impacts to be offset.

## Article 162: The Science of Gerontology

Gerontology studies aging, the accumulation of changes over time affecting all living organisms. Theories divide between programmed theories, suggesting predetermined senescence, and damage theories emphasizing accumulated molecular injuries. Neither fully explains complex aging.

Cellular hallmarks include genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, and loss of proteostasis. Mitochondrial dysfunction generates reactive oxygen species damaging cells. Senescent cells accumulate, secreting inflammatory factors that impair tissue function.

Organ system decline increases disease risk. Cardiovascular changes include arterial stiffening and reduced cardiac output. Immune system dysfunction impairs response to infection and tumors. Cognitive abilities vary; processing speed declines while crystallized knowledge may improve.

Longevity interventions show promise across species. Caloric restriction extends lifespan in organisms from yeast to primates. Senolytics clear damaged senescent cells. Rapamycin inhibits mTOR signaling associated with aging. Human trials explore these and other approaches to extend healthspan.
