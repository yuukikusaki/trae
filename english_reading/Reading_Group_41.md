## Article 121: The History of Agriculture

Agriculture, the cultivation of plants and animals for food and materials, began approximately 10,000 years ago during the Neolithic Revolution. Domestication of wheat, barley, rice, and maize transformed hunter-gatherers into farmers. Villages replaced nomadic camps; populations expanded.

Ancient civilizations developed irrigation, terraces, and crop rotation. Mesopotamian farmers constructed canals for irrigation. Egyptian agriculture depended on Nile flooding. Chinese farmers terraced hillsides to retain soil and water. Agricultural surplus enabled specialization, urbanization, and civilization.

The Agricultural Revolution of eighteenth-century Britain introduced crop rotation, selective breeding, and mechanization. Jethro Tull's seed drill sowed crops in rows. Enclosure movement consolidated fields for efficient farming. Productivity increased dramatically.

The Green Revolution of the mid-twentieth century developed high-yield crop varieties. Norman Borlaug's dwarf wheats fed millions in developing nations. Contemporary agriculture employs GPS guidance, genetic engineering, and precision irrigation. Challenges include environmental degradation, resource depletion, and food security.

## Article 122: The Science of Neurology

Neurology investigates the nervous system, including the brain, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves. Neurological disorders affect billions worldwide, causing disability and death. Research advances understanding of normal function while developing new treatments.

Neurodegenerative diseases involve progressive neuronal loss. Alzheimer's disease destroys memory and cognitive function through amyloid plaques and tau tangles. Parkinson's disease results from dopamine neuron degeneration, causing tremor and movement difficulties. Huntington's disease involves genetic mutation causing chorea and psychiatric symptoms.

Stroke occurs when blood supply to brain regions ceases, killing neurons. Ischemic strokes from clots; hemorrhagic strokes from bleeding. Rapid treatment with clot-busting drugs or thrombectomy can limit damage. Rehabilitation helps survivors regain function.

Epilepsy generates recurrent seizures from abnormal electrical discharges. Anticonvulsant medications control most cases. Surgical resection or device implantation helps refractory patients. Multiple sclerosis involves immune attack on myelin, disrupting nerve signals.

## Article 123: The Geography of Coastal Zones

Coastal zones, where land meets sea, support diverse ecosystems and dense human populations. Approximately forty percent of the global population lives within 100 kilometers of coasts. These areas provide critical habitat, food resources, and economic opportunities.

Shorelines feature various landforms shaped by wave, tide, and geological factors. Rocky cliffs resist erosion; sandy beaches accumulate sediment. Deltas form where rivers deposit sediments upon entering oceans. Coral reefs, built by colonial marine organisms, support exceptional biodiversity.

Coastal ecosystems include salt marshes, mangroves, estuaries, and beaches. Salt marshes shelter juvenile fish and filter pollutants. Mangrove forests stabilize shorelines and protect against storms. Estuaries, where fresh and salt water mix, provide nursery habitat for commercial fisheries.

Sea level rise threatens coastal communities and ecosystems. Global warming thermal expansion and ice melt raise ocean levels. Erosion accelerates; flooding increases. Managed retreat, hard protection, and nature-based solutions offer adaptation strategies.
